# Visual directions

Use these as starting points, not templates. A product can combine compatible ideas, but it should still have one clear visual identity.

## Editorial

A content-led direction with strong typographic hierarchy, deliberate whitespace, asymmetrical composition, and restrained rules or dividers. Use large type selectively and let headlines, images, and written content carry the page.

Good fit: portfolios, publishing, culture, education, research, and brand-led pages.

Avoid: turning every section into a magazine cover or sacrificing navigation and task clarity for dramatic typography.

## Warm minimalism

Quiet surfaces, warm neutrals, natural materials, subtle borders, and a small number of muted accents. The interface feels calm without becoming empty.

Good fit: personal tools, productivity products, wellness, creative software, and service businesses.

Avoid: low-contrast beige-on-beige text or adding decorative grain and blobs to manufacture warmth.

## Swiss / International

A disciplined grid, explicit alignment, functional typography, strong hierarchy, and carefully controlled color. Information is organized with precision.

Good fit: professional tools, data-heavy products, documentation, and dashboards.

Avoid: treating a grid as a substitute for hierarchy or making every page look like a poster.

## Neo-brutalist

Confident typography, visible structure, strong borders, hard-edged surfaces, and expressive but intentional color. The interface is direct and distinctive.

Good fit: independent products, creative tools, communities, and playful utilities.

Avoid: arbitrary color collisions, inaccessible contrast, or using harsh shadows on every element.

## Dark, low-luminance

Deep neutral backgrounds, layered surfaces with subtle separation, restrained highlights, and carefully tuned text contrast. It should feel designed for sustained use, not simply inverted from a light theme.

Good fit: developer tools, media tools, monitoring, and products used in low-light environments.

Avoid: pure black everywhere, neon accents on every control, and weak secondary text.

## Quiet premium

Precise spacing, confident typography, material-aware surfaces, subtle detailing, and a limited palette. Quality comes from proportion and consistency rather than ornament.

Good fit: specialist services, premium products, finance, and high-trust brands.

Avoid: gold accents and oversized serif headlines as shortcuts for luxury.

## Playful utility

Friendly geometry, clear labels, expressive illustrations or icons, and small moments of personality. The interface remains task-focused.

Good fit: learning tools, lightweight utilities, family products, and approachable consumer apps.

Avoid: making every element cute, using emoji as a substitute for a visual system, or hiding important actions in playful interactions.

## Technical / utilitarian

Compact but readable information, clear status indicators, tabular alignment, restrained color, and predictable interaction patterns.

Good fit: developer tools, admin interfaces, operations software, and complex workflows.

Avoid: unnecessary density, tiny text, or exposing internal implementation details as user-facing UI.

## Choosing a direction

Base the choice on the product's purpose, audience, content, and usage context. If two directions are plausible, present concise options with concrete differences in typography, palette, density, and composition. Do not present style labels alone as meaningful choices.
